Discovery and User Research Fundamentals

The Importance of Discovery in Product Management

Discovery is the foundation of successful product management. By taking the time to understand user needs and validate ideas before building, teams can avoid costly mistakes, reduce risk, and create solutions that truly make a difference. Investing in discovery at the start of the product process leads to better outcomes for both users and organizations.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to achieve the following learning objectives:

  1. Explain why discovery comes first in product management.
  2. Distinguish between problem space and solution space.
  3. Select and apply common user research methods.
  4. Conduct and analyze user interviews.
  5. Write actionable problem statements.

When discovery is skipped

The Mwangaza team once launched a quiz feature they thought students would love. But after weeks of development, hardly anyone used it. The team realized they hadn’t talked to students first to understand their real needs. As one team member put it: “Building the wrong thing is the costliest mistake.”

Discovery comes before solutions. It’s how product managers avoid costly mistakes by understanding real user needs before building.

Introduction to Problem and Solution Spaces

Understanding both the problem space and the solution space is crucial in product development. This process helps teams identify real user needs and create effective solutions. Let's explore the key steps involved.

Understand the Problem

Begin by learning about user pain points and needs. For instance, the Mwangaza team interviewed students to discover why they struggled to complete assignments. This step ensures you are addressing real issues.

Define the Opportunity

Clearly frame what your product could address. In the Mwangaza example, the team realized that students needed reminders and easier access to resources. Defining the opportunity helps focus your efforts on impactful solutions.

Explore Solutions

Brainstorm and test possible solutions to the defined problem. The Mwangaza team prototyped a notification feature and collected feedback before moving forward with development. This iterative approach ensures the solution meets user needs.

User Research Methods

Expand each tab to compare four key user research methods. Learn when to use each, typical sample sizes, and a risk to watch out for.

Interviews involve one-on-one conversations to deeply explore user experiences and motivations. They are best used early in discovery to uncover needs and pain points.

Typical sample size is 5–10 users. A risk is asking leading questions that bias responses.

Surveys collect structured data from a larger group, making them useful for validating trends or quantifying needs. They work well after initial interviews have identified key topics.

Sample sizes often range from 30–100 responses. A risk is poorly worded questions leading to unclear results.

Usability tests observe users as they interact with a prototype or product. They help identify where users struggle and what can be improved.

Sample size is usually 5–8 users per round. A risk is testing with users who aren’t representative of your target audience.

Analytics review uses data from product usage to spot patterns and issues. It’s effective for understanding what users do, not just what they say.

Sample size depends on your user base. A risk is misinterpreting data without context from qualitative research.

Running a good interview

Effective user interviews rely on a few key rules: ask open questions, focus on what users have actually done, and allow silence after answers. These techniques help you uncover honest insights and avoid assumptions, leading to more reliable findings.

The best interview questions are open and focused on what users have done, not what they say they might do.

From Notes to Insight: Turning Raw Data into Actionable Insights

See how raw interview notes are transformed into actionable insights. Follow each step using examples from Mwangaza’s research.

{{tl.label}}

Step 1 Capture Quotes

The team writes down exact phrases from students, such as "I forget to check the app for assignments."

Step 2 Group into Themes

Quotes are sorted into clusters, like reminders, navigation, and motivation.

Step 3 Name the Pattern

Each cluster is given a theme title, such as "Need for reminders."

Step 4 Write the Insight

The team summarizes: "Students need timely reminders to help them complete assignments."

Writing Problem Statements

Clear problem statements help teams focus on solving the right issues. Use the standard template to turn insights into actionable statements, and see how it works in practice.

[User] needs a way to [need] because [insight].

This template ensures the problem is user-centered and based on real findings.

A Mwangaza student needs a way to remember assignment deadlines because they often forget to check the app.

This example shows how a specific user need is linked to an insight from research.

A Mwangaza teacher needs a way to track student progress because it’s hard to know who is falling behind.

This example demonstrates how the template applies to different user roles.
